## Preamble

Ansai builds infrastructure.
Infrastructure companies are built differently from
consumer apps. They grow slower, earn deeper trust,
and command better retention.

This document governs how Ansai thinks about money —
how we earn it, how we allocate it, how we price,
and how we structure equity.

This document inherits from GID-001 and GWP-001.
The financial principles here are expressions of deeper
convictions — that data belongs to the institution,
that we earn our welcome, that depth matters more
than breadth. Finance is not separate from values.
It is how values become sustainable.

---

## 01 — The three financial principles

**Depth over breadth.**
Ten institutions that depend on an Ansai product daily
are worth more than a hundred that log in monthly.
Revenue quality matters more than revenue quantity
at this stage.
An institution that depends on Ansai and pays a fair
price is more valuable than one that was priced
aggressively and churns.

**Transparency always.**
Every person who joins Ansai knows the financial
situation honestly.
Revenue, runway, burn, costs — shared with the team.
A team that knows the financial reality makes better
decisions than one that does not.
This is not optional. It is a Pamoja requirement.

**Infrastructure pricing.**
We do not price like a SaaS tool.
We price like infrastructure — predictable, affordable
relative to the value delivered, structured around
the institution's actual operating cycles.
A school pays in terms. A farm pays around harvests.
Billing must align with how institutions actually
receive and manage money.

## 02 — Product line cost centres

Every Ansai product line operates as its own cost centre
from its first transaction.
This is a bookkeeping discipline, not a legal structure.

It enables clean financial history per product line,
supports future separation accounting, and makes
product-level profitability visible at all times.

### Cost centre structure

| Category | Definition |
|----------|------------|
| Revenue | All subscription fees, implementation fees, and transaction fees directly attributable to the product line. |
| Direct costs | Hosting, storage, third-party APIs, SMS, and any infrastructure costs directly attributable to the product line. |
| Shared costs | Ansai Group overhead — legal, accounting, founder salary — allocated to each product line proportionally. Redistributed as additional product lines launch. |
| Reporting | Monthly P&L per product line. Quarterly review against the financial model. Visible to all team members within the product line. |

## 03 — Pricing philosophy

Every Ansai product line is priced for the African
institutional market it serves.
Not borrowed from a Western SaaS pricing model.
Not adjusted from an enterprise software contract.
Designed from first principles around what the
institution can actually afford and what the product
is worth to them operationally.

### Pricing principles

**Price against the cost we replace.**
Not the cost of the software we compete with.
Identify the real operational cost the product
eliminates — staff time, errors, delays, lost data —
and price against that value.

**Align with the institution's operating cycles.**
Schools collect fees in three terms.
Farms harvest seasonally.
Subscription billing must align with how institutions
actually receive and manage money.
Annual billing with two months free is the standard
for EduManage. Equivalent logic applies to each
product line based on its sector's operating rhythm.

**Pricing tiers must reflect real budget differences.**
Government versus private.
Large versus small.
Urban versus rural.
Without compromising revenue quality.

**Expansion revenue is the model.**
An institution that onboards for one module should
naturally expand to others.
Pricing must make expansion easy, not painful.
Every tier should have a visible and affordable
next step above it.

**Never price yourself out of trust.**
In the first twelve months with any institution,
the goal is institutional dependency, not maximum revenue.
Get them dependent first. Price fairly always.
Do not extract value faster than you have created it.

## 04 — Equity philosophy

Equity conversations are the ones founders avoid longest.
Avoiding them creates confusion, resentment, and legal risk.
Ansai resolves them before they arise.

### The structure

| Element | Standard |
|---------|----------|
| Founder equity | Founder holds controlling equity in Ansai Technologies. Specific percentage determined by legal incorporation structure. |
| Employee equity pool | A defined percentage reserved for employees before any external investment. Established at incorporation, not at first hire. |
| Vesting schedule | Four-year vesting with a one-year cliff. Standard for all equity grants without exception. |
| Cliff rationale | The one-year cliff is not punitive. It is the minimum period to determine whether a hire is genuinely contributing to the mission. Equity without a cliff is equity without accountability. |
| Dilution transparency | Every equity holder is told what dilution means before they accept their grant. When Ansai raises external capital, existing equity is diluted. This is expected and explained in advance. |
| Departure protocol | Unvested equity returns to the pool on departure. Vested equity is retained. The distinction between voluntary departure and termination for cause is documented in the employment agreement. |

### The honest compensation conversation

Every offer to a new hire includes an explicit statement:
Ansai is paying below-market salaries until the first
external funding round.

This is not hidden. It is stated clearly at the beginning
of every conversation.
A candidate who cannot accept this should not receive
an offer, and the conversation ends there without
resentment from either side.

Equity compensates the gap between what we pay and
what the market pays.
This only works if the equity grant is real,
the vesting terms are fair, and the financial situation
is communicated honestly.
All three are required simultaneously.

## 05 — Capital allocation

### Priority order for deploying capital

| Priority | Category | Description |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| 1 | Product infrastructure | Domain, hosting, email, storage, CI/CD tooling. The foundation that makes everything else possible. First call on any available capital. |
| 2 | Direct institution costs | Onboarding visits, demo environment, marketing site. The tools for earning the first paying institutions. |
| 3 | People | The first hires. Paid below market with equity. Equity compensates the gap. |
| 4 | Growth | Integration costs, partnership development, marketing. Only after the product is stable and the first institutions are paying. |
| 5 | Ansai Group overhead | Legal, accounting, compliance. Minimum viable at each stage. Never over-invested before the product and team are in place. |

### Cloud credits strategy

Ansai actively pursues cloud startup credits before
spending any cash on infrastructure.

Google for Startups, Microsoft Founders Hub, AWS Activate,
Anthropic for Startups — all targeted before any
infrastructure spend.

No production institutional data on development accounts.
Production moves to approved credits or pay-as-you-go
when the first institution onboards.

Apply for credits before building anything agent-heavy.
The Anthropic for Startups program specifically reduces
the cost of Ansai Insight development significantly.

## 06 — Financial reporting standard

Every Ansai product line reports these seven numbers monthly.
No exceptions.
The team sees these numbers.
Investors — when they exist — see these numbers.
The founder sees these numbers every week.

| Metric | Definition |
|--------|------------|
| MRR | Monthly Recurring Revenue. The most important number after weekly active institutions. |
| Burn rate | Total monthly cash outflow. Cash in bank divided by burn rate equals runway in months. |
| Runway | How many months of operating capital remain at current burn. Must be visible at all times. |
| CAC | Customer Acquisition Cost. What does it cost to onboard one paying institution — including Mshauri Engineer time? |
| LTV | Lifetime Value per institution. Average monthly revenue multiplied by average institution tenure. |
| Churn rate | Percentage of institutions that cancel per month. The most important retention signal. |
| Collection rate | Where applicable — the transaction completion rate across all institutions on the platform. The product's impact metric. |

### The financial pulse in Ansai Insight

These seven numbers surface in Ansai Insight's
CFO view automatically.
The CFO reads them without requiring an engineer
to translate or prepare a report.
One view. Seven numbers. Always current.

## 07 — The financial philosophy and the mission

Money is not the mission.
The mission is the operational infrastructure
of African institutions.
Money is what makes the mission sustainable.

A product that cannot sustain itself financially
eventually fails the institutions depending on it —
which is a mission failure, not just a business failure.

Financial discipline is therefore a moral obligation,
not just a commercial one.

Pricing fairly, allocating capital wisely, tracking
the seven numbers honestly, and communicating the
financial reality transparently — these are not
separate from the values in GWP-001 and GCC-001.
They are expressions of them.

An institution that depends on Ansai must be able
to depend on Ansai being there in five years.
That requires financial sustainability.
Financial sustainability requires financial discipline.
Financial discipline is therefore part of Utu Engineering —
building with full awareness of the human being
who will still need this product in five years.
